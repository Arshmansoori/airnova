    02 · THE SCALE

    Understand the AQI scale — what every colour means

    In India, AQI runs from 0 to 500 and is divided into 6 bands. This is the official scale of the CPCB (Central Pollution Control Board) — the same scale AirNova uses. Note: AQI is set by the worst pollutant, and in Delhi NCR winters that is usually PM2.5 — the ultra-fine particles that can travel deep into the lungs and into the bloodstream.

    GoodAQI 0–50

    Health impact: Negligible. The air is clean and safe for everyone.

    What to do: Everything as normal — outdoor exercise, play, windows open. Make the most of days like this.

    SatisfactoryAQI 51–100

    Health impact: Most people feel nothing. Very sensitive people may have mild breathing discomfort.

    What to do: Normal routine. People with asthma should keep their inhaler with them and watch for symptoms.

    ModerateAQI 101–200

    Health impact: Children, older adults and people with breathing or heart conditions may start to have difficulty breathing. Healthy people are usually fine.

    What to do: Sensitive groups should reduce long and intense outdoor activity. Others can continue as normal, but avoid heavy outdoor exercise for more than 2 hours.

    PoorAQI 201–300

    Health impact: After long periods outdoors, even healthy people can develop breathing discomfort, eye irritation and a sore throat. Existing conditions can get worse.

    What to do: Wear an N95 if you must go out. Move outdoor exercise indoors. Children and older adults should keep time outside to a minimum.

    Very PoorAQI 301–400

    Health impact: Long exposure can cause respiratory illness in everyone; the effect on heart and lung patients is much stronger.

    What to do: Stop all outdoor exercise. Go out only for essential work, with a fit-tested N95. Keep windows closed at home and the purifier on.

    SevereAQI 401–500

    Health impact: Serious health effects are possible even for healthy people. This is close to a health emergency.

    What to do: Stay indoors. If you must go out, wear an N95 and keep it as short as possible. Do not send children outside at all. See a doctor promptly if symptoms appear.

    03 · MASKS

    Mask guide — which mask, when, and how

    PM2.5 particles are roughly 30 times finer than a human hair. A plain cloth mask or a scarf cannot stop them — it only blocks larger dust. A pollution mask works on two things: the filter (does it trap particles?) and the fit (does air leak in from the edges?). If either one is weak, the mask is only for show.

    BEST CHOICE

    N95 / KN95 / FFP2

    With a correct fit, it blocks at least 95% of fine particles, including PM2.5. This is the right choice for polluted days.

    • Always check for N95, KN95 or FFP2 marking on the pack
    • A head-strap model seals better than ear loops
    • Wear this if you go out when AQI is above 200
    LIMITED PROTECTION

    Surgical mask (3-ply)

    Some protection against droplets and coarse dust, but it leaks heavily at the edges — do not rely on it against PM2.5.

    • Acceptable for short trips when AQI is below 100
    • Use once and discard — do not wash or reuse
    • Wearing it over an N95 adds no extra benefit
    NOT FOR POLLUTION

    Cloth mask / scarf / dupatta

    Can protect against dust and sun, but PM2.5 passes straight through. Do not depend on these on smog days.

    • Fine only on clean days with AQI below 50
    • A valved mask does not filter exhaled air — it does not protect others in a crowd

    The right fit in 5 steps — without fit, a mask is useless

    Wash your hands, then pick up the mask

    Do not touch the inner surface of the mask. Hold it by the straps and put it on.

    Cover nose, mouth and chin fully

    The mask should run from above the nose to below the chin. Nose out = protection gone.

    Press the nose clip with both fingers

    Mould the metal strip to the shape of your nose — pinching with one finger is not enough.

    Do a fit test

    Breathe out sharply — if air leaks from the edges or your glasses fog up, adjust the mask. When you breathe in, the mask should pull in slightly.

    If you have a beard, take note

    A beard breaks the seal — air enters from the edges. Keep the mask line trimmed, or accept that protection will be reduced.

    When should you replace a mask?

    • Breathing through it takes noticeably more effort than before
    • The mask is damp, dirty, torn or loose
    • As a rule, replace an N95 after 40–50 hours of total use
    • Between uses, store it in a clean paper envelope — not in a pocket or plastic bag
    • Washing, spraying or sun-sanitising an N95 damages its filter

    Special points for children’s masks

    • Children under 2 should wear no mask at all — there is a risk of suffocation
    • Buy child-size KN95/N95 — an adult mask will leak
    • Children touch their masks constantly; build the hand-washing habit
    • No mask during play and running — at that point, stop outdoor play itself
    04 · EXERCISE

    Outdoor exercise — when it’s a yes, and when it’s a straight no

    During exercise you breathe 5–10 times faster, and through your mouth — bypassing the nose’s natural filter. That means while running you pull in many times more polluted air than a person sitting still. This is why the exercise decision changes first as AQI rises.

    The truth about the morning walk

    In winter, pollution in Delhi NCR is at its worst between 5 and 9 AM — cold air traps polluted particles near the ground (temperature inversion). The old belief that “morning air is the cleanest” works in reverse during smog season.

    When is the best time?

    AQI is often at its lowest between 12 and 3 PM, because sunlight mixes the air. If you need to pick an exercise hour, check the hourly AQI for your area on AirNova and choose the hour when AQI is lowest.

    Place matters too

    Running beside a busy road is the worst option — vehicle exhaust goes straight into your breathing. Choose the inner part of a large park, at least 200–300 metres from traffic. If you use a gym, pick one that runs an air purifier.

    05 · CHILDREN

    Special advice for children

    Children are at the highest risk from pollution: their lungs are still developing (growth continues until about age 18), they breathe in more air relative to their body weight than adults, and they spend more time playing outdoors while breathing through their mouths. That is why the rules for children are stricter than for adults.

    AQI 101–200

    They can play, but reduce the time

    • Limit outdoor play to 1 hour
    • Keep a child with asthma or allergies indoors
    • If coughing or breathlessness appears after play, stop immediately
    AQI 201–300

    No outdoor play — pay attention at school

    • Outdoor sports, PT and running completely stopped — speak to the school
    • Use a child-size N95 for the school commute
    • Classroom windows should stay closed; a room with a purifier is best
    AQI 300+

    Keep them indoors

    • Outdoor play, the park, cycling — everything stopped
    • Fill the day with indoor games, study and drawing
    • Persistent cough, a whistling sound (wheeze) or fast breathing — see a doctor the same day

    5 habits for parents that actually work

    • Check the AQI every morning with your tea — and plan the day around it.
    • Always keep a spare mask in the school bag; children lose masks.
    • Keep the nebuliser / inhaler ready on your doctor’s advice — so you are not running to a pharmacy on a bad day.
    • Keep the nose clean: wash hands and face when they come home, and change their clothes — dust clings to fabric.
    • Do not dismiss a child’s cough as “just a habit” — a cough lasting more than 2 weeks needs a doctor.
    06 · SENSITIVE GROUPS

    Sensitive groups — older adults, patients and pregnant women

    The same AQI does not affect everyone equally. For the groups below, “Moderate” AQI is what “Poor” is for a healthy adult — which is why their answers in the advisor tool tighten one band earlier.

    Older adults (60+)

    • Stop the morning walk above AQI 150; walk indoors or in the lower-AQI afternoon hour
    • Above AQI 200, go out only for essential work, with an N95
    • Do not ignore breathlessness, heaviness in the chest or dizziness as “just age” — these can be pollution symptoms
    • Take BP and diabetes medicines on time; BP can fluctuate more in polluted weeks

    Asthma / COPD / Allergies

    • Keep the rescue inhaler in your pocket at all times — you may need it as soon as AQI crosses 100
    • Get your “bad-day action plan” written by your doctor in advance: which medicine to increase, and when
    • Above AQI 200, keep going out to a minimum; above 300, stay indoors with the purifier on
    • If the rescue inhaler is suddenly needed more often (more than twice a week), see your doctor — it signals losing control

    Heart patients / High BP

    • PM2.5 enters the bloodstream and increases the load on the heart — above AQI 200, avoid heavy work and outdoor exertion
    • Chest pain or pressure, or pain spreading to the arm, jaw or back, must never be dismissed as pollution — treat it as an emergency
    • Cold plus smog together make early-morning outings the riskiest

    Pregnant women

    • Long pollution exposure can affect the baby’s development — above AQI 200, keep time outdoors to a minimum
    • If you must go out, wear a well-fitted N95 and avoid crowded, traffic-heavy routes
    • Run the purifier in the bedroom at home
    • Do not take any medicine or supplement without your doctor

    Outdoor workers

    Delivery riders, drivers, traffic police, construction workers — you cannot simply stay home. For you, protection is the only way:

    • A well-fitted N95 for the entire shift — this is your biggest protection
    • Take breaks away from smoke, in an enclosed place; breathe clean air there with the mask off
    • Drink plenty of water; if your eyes burn, rinse them with clean water
    • If cough or breathing trouble persists, get a medical check-up — do not compromise it for work

    Pets count too

    • Dogs’ and cats’ lungs are also affected by PM2.5
    • Above AQI 200, keep walks short and no running at all
    • Do not take elderly pets or pets with breathing issues out above 300
    • After a walk, wipe the paws and face with a clean cloth
    07 · INDOOR AIR

    Indoor air — purifiers, windows and everyday smoke

    When AQI outside is above 300, your home is your protection — but only if the air inside is actually clean. Even in a closed home, outside air slowly seeps in, and indoor smoke (cooking, incense, cleaning chemicals) makes it worse.

    Air purifier — choosing right

    • Buy one with a HEPA filter (H13 or H14) — that is what traps PM2.5
    • The CADR rating should exceed your room size: as a rule of thumb, aim for CADR (in m³/h) above roughly two-thirds of the room’s area in sq. ft. — higher CADR means faster cleaning
    • Avoid “ioniser / ozone” purifiers — ozone itself harms the lungs
    • Replace the filter every 6–12 months (always check after Delhi’s smog season)
    • Run the purifier in the bedroom with the door closed — one small purifier cannot cover a whole house

    Windows — open or closed?

    • AQI below 100: open the windows and let fresh air in — CO2 and humidity build up in a closed home
    • AQI 100–200: open only for the 1–2 hours when AQI is at its lowest
    • AQI above 200: windows and doors closed; a damp cloth along the gap under the door helps
    • AQI above 400: seal the home fully, purifier on high, postpone outdoor work

    Smoke inside the home

    • Incense, dhoop, candles, mosquito coils — all raise indoor PM2.5 quickly; stop or reduce them on smog days
    • Kitchen: always run the exhaust/chimney during tempering and deep-frying; this is daily exposure for the cook
    • Wet-mop instead of dry-sweeping — dry dust does not become airborne
    • No smoking indoors, ever — children are affected the most
    • If you vacuum, use a HEPA-filter model; sensitive people should stay in another room during cleaning

    No purifier? These low-cost measures work

    • Make one bedroom the “clean room” — seal the gaps in its doors and windows, and spend most of your time there.
    • DIY option: strapping a HEPA filter behind a box fan (a Corsi-Rosenthal box) can clean a room’s air considerably — take full care with electrical safety.
    • Wet-mop daily, and leave outdoor shoes and clothes at the door — less dust comes inside.
    • Plants are lovely for the home, but cleaning one room’s air would take hundreds of them — do not treat them as a purifier substitute.
    08 · FOOD & CARE

    Food, drink and caring for your body

    Understand this clearly: no food is a cure for pollution. But the right food supports the body’s ability to fight inflammation, and some habits genuinely reduce throat and lung discomfort.

    Add more of these to your plate

    • Vitamin C: amla, orange, lemon, guava, capsicum
    • Colourful vegetables and fruit: carrot, spinach, tomato — a natural source of antioxidants
    • Omega-3: walnuts, flaxseed, and fatty fish for those who eat it
    • Turmeric milk / ginger tea: soothes a sore throat

    Habits that bring relief

    • Drink plenty of water — dry air and mouth-breathing dry the throat further
    • Steam inhalation relieves a blocked nose and throat — but it does not “clean” dust that has entered the lungs
    • Salt-water gargles help with throat irritation
    • Full sleep: the body repairs itself in sleep; feeling more tired on smog days is common

    Avoid these

    • Smoking and second-hand smoke — a double attack on top of pollution
    • “Detox” medicines and supplement packs taken without a doctor — there is no evidence for them
    • Do not start antibiotics or a steroid inhaler on your own
    • Very heavy fried food can increase inflammation on smog days — lighter food is better
    09 · MEDICAL HELP

    When to see a doctor — symptoms and red flags

    A mild cough, burning eyes and a sore throat are common at high AQI and usually settle once you get clean air. But never dismiss the symptoms below with “it’s just pollution, it will pass.”

    Go to hospital / emergency immediately — these are red flags

    • Severe difficulty breathing — breathless even while sitting, speech broken by breathlessness
    • Chest pain or pressure, especially pain spreading to the arm, jaw or back
    • Lips or face turning blue
    • Coughing up blood
    • Asthma patients: no relief from the rescue inhaler, or the wheeze getting louder
    • In a child: the skin between the ribs sinking in while breathing, fast breathing, refusing milk/food
    • Dizziness, feeling faint or confused speech

    In this situation, do not wait at home — call 112 or go to the nearest hospital emergency.

    See a doctor within 2–3 days if:

    • A cough lasts more than 2 weeks, or the colour of phlegm changes
    • Breathing trouble returns every smog season — a lung function test (spirometry) may be needed
    • Eyes stay red and watery continuously
    • A child’s cough breaks their sleep at night
    • The need for the rescue inhaler keeps increasing

    Do not forget to ask your doctor

    • “What should my bad-AQI action plan be?” — get in writing when to increase medicines
    • “Is my inhaler technique correct?” — with wrong technique, the medicine never reaches the lungs
    • “Is the flu / pneumonia vaccine right for me?” — best asked before smog season
    • “Which medicines should I keep ready at home for my child?”
    10 · MYTHS vs FACTS

    Myths vs facts — the claims that are simply wrong

    Half-knowledge about pollution does the most harm. These are the misconceptions that come back every smog season:

    MYTH

    “We Delhi people are used to it — it doesn’t affect us”

    Fact: Habit only dulls the feeling, not the harm. Less eye irritation does not mean PM2.5 is not reaching your lungs and blood. Long-term exposure works silently — which is why “nothing happens to me” is the most dangerous mindset.

    MYTH

    “The air at 5 AM is the cleanest”

    Fact: In smog season it is the opposite. Overnight, cold air keeps pollution trapped near the ground; the morning hours are often the worst of the day. Decide your walk time by AQI, not by the clock.

    MYTH

    “Houseplants clean the air”

    Fact: Plants clean very slowly and very little — a real effect on one room would need hundreds of plants. Keep plants, but do not treat them as a substitute for a purifier or a closed window.

    MYTH

    “An expensive mask = more protection”

    Fact: Not the price — certification and fit are what matter. A correctly fitted ordinary N95 is a hundred times better than a loose designer mask. Check the pack for N95/KN95/FFP2 marking.

    MYTH

    “Steam cleans the lungs”

    Fact: Steam soothes the nose and throat, but it does not remove particles that have entered deep into the lungs. Fine for comfort — not as a “lung detox”.

    MYTH

    “Inside a car with the AC on, we are safe”

    Fact: A car’s cabin filter is usually not made for PM2.5. Keep recirculation mode on and windows closed — indoor pollution drops a lot, but not to zero. Wearing an N95 on a long drive is not wrong.

    11 · DAILY PLAN

    Your daily smog-day plan — morning to night

    On severe days, routine works better than panic. This is the checklist Delhi NCR families can adopt every smog season:

    • Check the AQI as soon as you wake up — on AirNova, see your area’s AQI and the lowest-AQI hour of the day; plan the day around it.
    • Decide exercise by AQI: above 200, cancel outdoor exercise and keep an indoor routine ready so the habit does not break.
    • Children’s school plan: mask in the bag, and speak to the school in advance about the sports period — no outdoor activity above 300.
    • Set up the home: above AQI 200, close the windows, run the purifier in the bedroom, wet-mop the floors.
    • If you must go out: fit-test and wear an N95, prefer inner lanes over traffic roads, and keep the time as short as possible.
    • Keep medicines ready: inhaler, allergy medicine and children’s syrup — with expiry dates checked — all in one place.
    • When you return home: wash hands and face, change out of outdoor clothes — dust travels with fabric all the way to the bed.
    • In the evening, check tomorrow: use AirNova’s 72-hour forecast to decide tomorrow’s outdoor plans in advance.

    12 · GLOSSARY

    A–Z glossary — every term in simple words

    AQI (Air Quality Index)
    A 0–500 number for how clean or dirty the air is. In India, CPCB’s 6 bands: Good, Satisfactory, Moderate, Poor, Very Poor, Severe.
    PM2.5
    Particles smaller than 2.5 microns — about 30 times finer than hair. They can pass from the lungs into the blood; the biggest enemy in Delhi’s smog.
    PM10
    Particles up to 10 microns — dust and road grit. They lodge in the nose and throat, causing cough and irritation.
    N95 / KN95 / FFP2
    Mask certification standards — a tested claim of blocking at least 95% of fine particles with a correct fit.
    HEPA Filter
    The purifier filter that traps 99.97% of particles down to 0.3 microns. Without HEPA, a purifier does half the job.
    CADR
    A number for how fast a purifier cleans air. The bigger the room, the bigger the CADR you need.
    COPD
    A chronic lung disease in which the airways gradually narrow; pollution is most dangerous for its patients.
    Spirometry
    A simple breathing test that measures lung strength. A doctor may order it for repeated cough or breathlessness.
    Temperature Inversion
    In winter, a layer of cold air traps pollution near the ground — which is why AQI is worst in the morning and at night.
    GRAP
    Graded Response Action Plan — the staged government restrictions in Delhi NCR as AQI rises (construction bans, school closures, and so on).
    Wheeze
    A whistling sound while breathing — a sign of narrowed airways; in asthma, act on it immediately.
    Rescue Inhaler
    The inhaler that gives immediate relief (such as salbutamol) — always keep it with you; needing it more often means the disease is going out of control.
    13 · FAQ

    Frequently asked questions

    Can I exercise while wearing a mask?+
    An N95 is manageable on a light walk, but during hard running or heavy exercise a mask makes breathing harder, and once it gets damp its protection falls too. The real rule: at an AQI where you would need a mask to run, you should not be exercising outdoors at all — switch indoors.
    Why is my area’s AQI different from the AQI on TV?+
    Every locality has its own AQI — near a road, near a park and in an industrial area, the difference at the same moment can be up to 100 points. TV shows a city-wide average. That is why you should check the AQI at your own GPS point — AirNova gives exactly this hyperlocal AQI with a 72-hour forecast.
    Can one N95 last the whole smog season?+
    No. As a rule, replace it after 40–50 hours of total use, or sooner if it becomes damp, dirty or loose, or breathing through it takes effort — whichever comes first. Washing or spraying a mask damages its filter.
    My child refuses to wear a mask — what should I do?+
    Build the habit instead of forcing it: practise playfully at home, get a child-size mask in a colour or design they like, and wear one yourself to show them. But remember — no mask at all for children under 2, and during play the right move is to stop the play, not to mask it.
    Is one purifier enough for the whole house?+
    Usually not. A purifier cleans only the room it runs in, with the door closed. Cover the bedroom first — that is where we breathe continuously for 7–8 hours. Then add a second one for the children’s room as needed.
    AQI is 150 — should I send my child to the park?+
    For a healthy child, up to 1 hour of light play is fine, but keep running-around play low. If the child has asthma or allergies, keep them indoors even at 150. Once AQI crosses 200, outdoor play stops for all children — the easiest rule to remember.
    If I never open windows on smog days, won’t the indoor air go stale?+
    Long closure does raise CO2 and humidity, so as soon as AQI drops below 100, open the windows and change the air. On in-between days (100–200), pick the cleanest 1–2 hours of the day. Above 200, keeping windows closed is still better — outdoor PM2.5 does more harm than indoor humidity.
